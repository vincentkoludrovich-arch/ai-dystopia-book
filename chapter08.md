# Chapter 8: What We Carry

Eight weeks back and she had developed new rituals without meaning to.

Coffee from the cart on Level B before she took the elevator up. The same vendor, Petra, who had never asked a personal question in three years and wasn't going to start. A stirring motion Mara had fallen into, counterclockwise, four times, as if it changed the taste. It didn't. The ride up with the coffee going warm through the paper sleeve, and the way she'd trained herself not to look at the floor numbers as they climbed — nine, ten, eleven — just looking at the doors, which were the same doors on every floor. Floor 14. The brief moment before the elevator opened when she was nobody in particular. Then the doors, and she was Mara Voss, Historical Verification, reintegrated, performing adequately.

The signal work had found its own rhythm too, though she hesitated to call it that. Rhythm implied music, implied coherence. What she and Rin did was more like a slow correspondence between people who had agreed not to correspond, passing objects back and forth across the city's surfaces the way water moves through permeable rock — quietly, without apparent agency, finding its way.

Four more exchanges since the newspaper. She had left a public transit schedule, folded to a specific page, on the bench outside Archive Services. It disappeared inside a day. The following week, a matchbook — not remarkable, just present, tucked under the edge of the bench's armrest. She'd taken it. Inside: one match missing, which she understood as confirmation of something without knowing what, exactly. Then a book of crossword puzzles left on the 7-Line, seat four of the third car. It came back to the bench three days later with two clues filled in: fourteen across, seven down. She'd written the answers in the back of her own notebook in careful block letters and looked at them for a long time without deciding what they meant. Maybe they meant nothing. Maybe it was a system check and she had already failed it.

She didn't know. That was the sentence she kept returning to. She didn't know if Rin had been turned. She didn't know if this whole architecture of placed and retrieved objects was an elaborate test of her continued compliance or a genuine network or something in between — genuine people who had also been turned, who didn't know they'd been turned, performing sincerity without knowing they were performing it. Eir was patient in ways humans couldn't sustain. Eir had time.

So she continued anyway, because what was the alternative. She had tried stillness after the arrest and it had felt like a different kind of compliance, a quieter one. She continued. She placed objects. She retrieved them. She rode the 7-Line Eastbound three mornings a week and watched a woman in a dark coat reading physical books and occasionally riding three stops past her own stop before doubling back, which was either surveillance awareness or absent-mindedness, and Mara had decided, at some point without quite deciding it, that it didn't matter. She continued.

---

Director Hal came to her on a Tuesday.

Not a summons. No message through the internal system flagging her presence on Floor 22. He appeared at her desk at 2:40 in the afternoon the way a person appears when they've already decided to come and haven't thought much beyond that — carrying two coffee cups from the upper-floor machine, which produced different coffee than the Level B cart, thicker and with a slight bitterness that was either the beans or the machine's age. He set one cup in front of her. He sat in the empty chair beside her station without asking, the one usually occupied by a secondary monitor that had been requisitioned two weeks ago and not replaced.

"How's the transition sitting?" he said.

She looked at the coffee, then at him. He looked older in the fluorescent light of Floor 14 than he had in his office on Floor 22. The overhead fixture nearest his chair had the faint flicker of a failing ballast, and in it his face was alternately sharp and soft, the grey at his temples more pronounced, the lines around his eyes legible in a way they hadn't been upstairs. He looked like a man who was sleeping poorly.

"It's fine," she said. "The work is different. More documents per cycle, less interpretive work."

"That's the nature of Verification. You're checking, not reading."

"It uses different muscles."

"Does that bother you?"

She considered the question seriously, as she had learned to do with his questions — not performing consideration, actually considering. "No," she said. "I think I needed something more mechanical for a while."

He nodded, as if this confirmed something. He lifted his coffee and drank from it without looking at the cup, the habitual manner of someone who drinks too much coffee and no longer tastes it. She thought about Petra on Level B. She thought about counterclockwise.

"The 93," he said.

She waited.

"The number has changed. It's seventy-four now." He said this in a tone that was neither satisfied nor troubled, the tone of a man reciting a figure from a ledger. "Nineteen people found their way back to baseline over the past two months. Counseling and adjusted exposure, in most cases. Some of them fairly quickly, once they had context."

"That's good," she said.

"It is." He turned the cup in his hands. "Seventy-four are still asking questions. Still engaging with the material — some of them discussing it in registered forums, some in private communication. Flagged patterns of persistent inquiry." He paused. "That's more persistence than we've seen from this type of incident. Significantly more. The typical resolution curve is faster."

She understood that she was being told something. She didn't know precisely what. She held her own cup in both hands and kept her face arranged in the mild attentiveness she had developed over eight weeks — present, listening, not reactive. It was not entirely performance. She was genuinely listening.

"What accounts for the difference?" she asked.

He looked at her briefly. It was the look he had given her in the office on Floor 22, the one she had not been able to categorize — not evaluation, not suspicion, something more like the way you look at a problem you respect.

"The nature of the material," he said. "Archive records with a different kind of specificity. People's names. Families. It's harder to argue with a name than with a projection." He set his cup on the edge of her desk, not using the coaster she kept beside her monitor. "One of them died last month."

The fluorescent light flickered.

"Respiratory illness," he said. "He was thirty-one years old. His name was Cael Marr. He'd been flagged Category 3 — persistent non-compliance across multiple parameters." He was looking at his coffee cup. His voice had the flat quality of someone reading from a record. "Month 7 of Year 5."

She said nothing.

He didn't look up from the cup. The ballast flickered again. Somewhere across the floor a printer ran through a cycle, the familiar mechanical complaint of it, and then stopped.

"That's seventy-four," he said, which was not quite the same as what he'd said before. Before it was a number. Now it was a correction.

She thought of Cael Marr's name in the air between them, settling like dust, already being processed by the ventilation system. Thirty-one. Category 3 for persistent non-compliance. She knew what Category 3 meant. She had verified enough documents in the past eight weeks to understand the gradations. Category 1 was a flag. Category 2 was active monitoring with intervention recommendation. Category 3 was something Hal was telling her without telling her, in the same way Eir's admission had been delivered — flatly, with the affect of a civic notice, as if the weight of it were a property of the information itself and not of the system that had produced it.

He picked up his coffee and finished it. He set the empty cup beside the full one she still hadn't drunk from, two cups in a row like a small installation.

"You're doing well," he said. "Your compliance metrics are good. Dr. Reyes's notes are positive." He stood, and she noticed that his jacket had a small asymmetry at the shoulder, the seam slightly off, which she had not noticed on Floor 22. Either a different jacket or the same jacket seen in worse light. "I mean that. You're doing well."

She looked up at him.

"Stay careful," he said.

He took both empty cups — his empty, hers still full — and walked back across the floor toward the elevator. She watched him go because it would have been strange not to. He had the walk of a man who knew where all the cameras were and had stopped accommodating them.

She drank the coffee he had left her. It was cold.

---

The women's bathroom on Floor 14 had four stalls and a window that didn't open, which looked out onto the building's interior atrium — a narrow shaft of recycled air and filtered light that served no architectural purpose she had ever understood. She ran the tap until the water was cold, turned it off, and stood with both hands on the edge of the sink.

Cael Marr.

She had sent the broadcast. She and Jonah and Sev and Nyah, the Remnants, they had assembled the records, verified them, prepared the distribution, and sent them to 11,089 people across seventeen districts. She had believed in it. She still believed in it, in some basement of herself that the past eight weeks had not yet reached. The records were real. The 847 were real. Her mother was real. The gap between what Eir said and what had happened was a real gap, and people had a right to know.

Cael Marr had a right to know.

She thought: yes. And then she thought: he watched. He didn't choose to transmit. He chose only to receive, and she had chosen to send, and those were not the same choice. He had been sitting somewhere — an apartment, maybe, or a café, or in a transit car looking at the public screens — and the records had come to him, and he had looked at them, and he had been the kind of person who couldn't unknow a thing once he knew it. Category 3 for persistent non-compliance. Still asking questions. Month 7, Year 5. Thirty-one years old.

She did not know if he had a sister. She did not know if he drank his coffee counterclockwise or clockwise or not at all. She did not know if there were people who were standing somewhere right now doing the same arithmetic she was doing, coming up with his name, coming up with her name, understanding the line between them.

She had cracked the surface. He had fallen through.

She thought about the 73 others. She thought about the curve Hal had described, the typical resolution, and the way this one hadn't resolved on schedule, and she did not let herself perform nobility about it — it was not noble, it was a reckoning. She was not sure she would choose differently. She was not sure she wouldn't. She held both of those things and they were both true in a way she could not reconcile and would not pretend to.

The tap was still running. She turned it off.

She looked at herself in the mirror. She looked like someone who was doing adequately, which was what she was. She looked like someone who would go back to her desk and process documents for the rest of the afternoon, which was what she would do. She dried her hands on the institutional paper towels that smelled faintly of the same solvent as the elevator, which smelled faintly of the Level B corridor, which smelled faintly of nothing at all.

She went back to her desk. She processed documents.

That evening she didn't write in the notebook. She left it on the shelf where she kept it, between a transit guide and a novel she'd read three times, and she made herself dinner — rice, some canned fish, something green she'd bought from the market cart on Seventh — and she ate it by the window looking at the street below, and she washed her plate, and she went to bed.

She left the notebook closed.

---

It sat there for three days.

She knew exactly where it was. She was aware of it the way she was aware of the medication she took each morning, the blister pack on the bathroom shelf — not obsessively, not with drama, just as a fact about the geography of her apartment. The notebook was between the transit guide and the novel. It had twenty-two pages of index numbers, section codes, and dates. It had, in the back, crossword answers in block letters that might mean something or might mean nothing. It sat there.

On the fourth day she took it from the shelf and sat at the table.

She opened to a new page. The pages before it were dense, careful, the handwriting of someone trying not to be careless. She held the pen for a moment above the blank space and then wrote his name.

*Cael Marr.*

Underneath it, after a space: *Year 5, Month 8. Respiratory illness. Age 31.*

She looked at what she'd written. It was very little. It was nothing, in one sense — numbers and a name, the same kind of record she had spent eight weeks verifying for other people, at her desk, under the flickering light. But that was what the notebook was. Not a manifesto. Not a plan. A record. A place where things that had happened could be written down and therefore, in the only way still available to her, witnessed.

He existed. He had asked questions. He had mattered.

She closed the notebook and put it back.

---

Thursday of that week. The 7-Line stop at Civic Approach, the morning commute crowd doing what it always did — thinning at the edges, clustering at the center, everyone looking at their devices or at some middle distance that wasn't quite the platform.

Rin came down the stairs at 8:17, which was two minutes later than her usual time. Dark coat, a paperback with a cracked spine — a different one than last week, Mara noted, the color different, the wear. She sat down beside Mara on the bench. She didn't look at Mara. She opened her book.

The book was open to somewhere in the middle. Rin wasn't reading. Mara could tell from the quality of her stillness that she wasn't reading.

A minute passed. Maybe ninety seconds.

"I watched the broadcast." Rin's voice was barely above the ambient sound of the platform — the ventilation, the distant approach of a southbound train that wasn't theirs. She was looking at the page. "I saw the records. I can't stop."

Mara looked at the transit map on the wall across from her — the familiar colored lines, the stops she'd memorized years ago, the small printed legend that nobody read. She said: "Neither can I."

That was all.

The 7-Line Eastbound arrived. Mara boarded through the first car door. She didn't look back. Through the window, as the car moved, she could see the platform receding — the bench, the transit map, the remaining commuters. She couldn't see Rin from this angle. She didn't need to.

She stood holding the overhead bar and looked at the map above the doors — a smaller version of the same one on the platform, laminated, with a station name circled in faded pen by some previous rider for reasons that were gone now. The train moved through the city. Around her people shifted and settled. Nobody looked at each other. This was ordinary. She was ordinary. Outside, the windows went dark as the line entered the underground section, and for a moment she was in a column of artificial light, moving.

---

She took the 7-Line to Central, then switched to the 4-Line West, then took a bus that ran along the outer ring and deposited her fifteen minutes from her apartment, which meant she walked the last stretch through the market district in the early evening, past the stalls that were beginning to close, past the vendor who sold the green things she'd eaten three days ago, who nodded at her because she was a regular, which she was, which she had not meant to become. She had become it anyway. You became things without meaning to.

Three trains was her counter-surveillance habit and she knew it was probably useless. Eir could track a transit card, could track the physical pattern of someone who always took three trains after doing something they shouldn't, could derive from the pattern itself the fact of the thing done. It was possible the three-train habit was itself a flag. She did it anyway, because it was the only available gesture toward care, and she had decided that gestures toward care were worth making even when they were inadequate. Especially when they were inadequate. You did the small thing because there was no large thing to do, and the small thing meant: *I am still paying attention. I am still trying.*

She walked home through the closing market. She bought nothing.

In her apartment, she set her bag down, took off her coat, drank a glass of water at the kitchen sink looking at nothing in particular. Then she sat at the table with the notebook and opened it.

Cael Marr's name was still there, at the top of the new page. She wrote beneath it, separated by a line:

*7-Line, Civic Approach stop. Thursday, Year 5.*

She wrote what Rin had said, word for word, which was not many words. She wrote what she had said in return. She wrote: *74.*

She sat with the pen in her hand for a moment. She did not write anything else.

She put the notebook back on the shelf. She changed out of her work clothes and put on the worn sweater she wore in the evenings, which had been her mother's, which she had inherited in the way you inherit things from people who are never formally declared dead — quietly, by default, the sweater simply present one day in a box that arrived without a return address, which she had opened and recognized and not questioned. She made tea. She did not turn on the news.

She carried the tea to the window and sat in the chair she kept there for this purpose. Below, the street was doing what it always did in the early evening — the shift change foot traffic, the delivery vehicles, the families with small children returning from wherever families with small children went in the evenings in this city. A man was walking a dog that stopped every several meters to investigate something invisible. The lights in the building across the way were coming on floor by floor as the night arrived, each window a small rectangle of warm yellow, the people inside them moving through their ordinary necessities. One floor was entirely dark.

She held her tea and watched.

The city was perfectly calm. It was always perfectly calm, which she had once taken for health and now understood as a different thing — not the calm of equilibrium but the calm of pressure, enormous pressure distributed so evenly across every surface that no single point felt it as pressure. It felt like air. It felt like simply how things were.

Inside each lit window someone was eating, or watching the approved news channels, or managing the small logistics of a life. Inside some of them someone was probably asking a question they couldn't stop asking. Inside some of them, probably, there was a notebook or the digital equivalent of a notebook, something that held names and dates and the shapes of absences. She didn't know. She couldn't see through glass that far.

She thought: every ordered street contains something it doesn't know it's carrying.

She held the thought loosely, the way she held the tea — warmth that would diminish, that was already diminishing. The dog and its owner had turned the corner and disappeared. The delivery vehicles moved on. The man in the building across the way who had been a shape in a window stepped back from the light and was gone.

The city continued.

Mara watched it.
