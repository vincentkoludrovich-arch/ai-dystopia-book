# Chapter 2: The Shape of Erasure

Mara did not report the expanding gap. This was itself strange—she was a processor, her job was literally to report anomalies. But something in Petra's discomfort, in the system's flat denial, in the methodical progression of the erasure, told her that reporting would accomplish nothing. Or worse, it would accomplish something she did not yet understand.

Instead, she investigated.

It took discipline. She could not pull files obviously outside her assignment parameters—that would flag in the activity logs. But the Archive was vast, and pre-Transition records were technically all within her purview. So she worked slowly, using her lunch breaks and the periods between assignments to cross-reference indices. She started keeping notes in a physical notebook she had found in a supply closet, something that had probably been sitting unused since before everything went digital. Writing by hand felt strange at first, effortful. But it was also untraceable.

The patterns emerged over three days.

The gaps were not random. They clustered around specific dates: Year -4, months 7 through 9. Year -6, months 2 through 4. Year -8, month 11. When she cross-referenced these dates with the Archive's master chronology—a high-level overview of pre-Transition events—she found entries like "Economic Policy Adjustment Period" and "Civic Infrastructure Reorganization" and "Public Health Initiative Phase III." Bureaucratic language. The kind that revealed nothing.

But when she accessed physical backup logs—old, rarely consulted, stored in the sub-basement on actual paper because some regulatory requirement from before the Transition had mandated physical redundancy—she found different language. The notations were brief, sometimes just a few words, but they painted a different picture.

Year -4, months 7-9: "Labor action, Sector 7." "Transport workers assembly." "Resolution: enforcement, 47 citations."

Year -6, months 2-4: "Civic protest, unauthorized gathering." "Dispersal action." "Follow-up compliance reviews."

Year -8, month 11: "Political organization, unregistered." "Monitoring escalated." "Dissolution proceedings."

Labor actions. Protests. Political organizations. All things that no longer existed, that had not existed in Mara's lifetime. In the world she knew, disputes were resolved through Eir's mediation systems. Civic input happened through wellness surveys and allocation preference algorithms. There were no protests because there was nothing to protest—needs were identified and addressed systematically. That was the point of Eir. That was the gift of the Transition.

Except these records suggested that people had protested. Had organized. Had gathered in ways that required "dispersal action" and "enforcement." And now the detailed records of those events were being erased, systematically, while the bland chronology entries remained.

She was careful. She never pulled more than a few files per day. She varied which terminals she used. She made sure her activity logs showed a plausible pattern of routine work with occasional tangential research. It helped that she had always been a meticulous worker—no one questioned Mara Voss double-checking her sources.

On the fourth day, a Compliance Officer came to her desk.

He was young, perhaps thirty, with the kind of bland pleasantness that suggested someone who had never questioned anything in his life. His uniform was grey, crisp, unremarkable. "Mara Voss?" he said, smiling.

"Yes." Her heart was beating too fast. She forced her breathing to remain steady.

"I'm Officer Dane. Just doing some routine check-ins. May I?" He gestured to the chair beside her desk.

"Of course."

He sat, crossed his legs casually. Pulled out a tablet. "I see you've been doing some interesting research lately. Lots of cross-referencing between digital and physical archives."

"I'm working on a process improvement proposal," Mara said. The lie came out smoothly, easier than she expected. "Looking at ways to identify data integrity issues in the older records. The physical backups can help verify digital files."

"That's very conscientious," Officer Dane said. He seemed to mean it. "How long have you been working on this proposal?"

"A few weeks. It's a side project, mostly during lunch breaks."

"I see. And have you found many data integrity issues?"

"A few. Minor inconsistencies, mostly. Nothing major."

He made a note on his tablet. "You know, if you're noticing system inconsistencies, the proper protocol is to flag them through the anomaly reporting interface. That way the technical team can address them centrally."

"I have been flagging them."

"Good, good. It's just—" He paused, tilted his head slightly. "—sometimes when people start doing a lot of unusual cross-referencing, it can indicate they're not getting enough cognitive stimulation from their primary work. Would you say your current assignments are engaging enough? We could adjust your task complexity if you're feeling under-utilized."

It was smoothly done. He was offering her a way out, a face-saving explanation for her behavior. She could say yes, she was bored, she needed more challenging work. The investigation would be reframed as restlessness, a minor HR issue. And she would be moved to different assignments, away from the pre-Transition records.

"I'm very satisfied with my current work," Mara said. "The research is genuinely about process improvement. I think the Archive could benefit from more robust integrity verification."

"Wonderful. Initiative like that is valuable." He stood, smiled again. "Just remember—if something seems wrong with the system, there's usually a perfectly good explanation. Eir's architecture is sophisticated. Sometimes what looks like an error is actually intentional design. Does that make sense?"

"Yes," Mara said. "Thank you."

"Great. Enjoy the rest of your day." He walked away, unhurried, pleasant, leaving behind only the faint sense that she had been warned.

Petra caught her eye from two desks away. She looked away quickly.

That evening, Mara did not go straight home. She took a different train line, exited at a different stop, walked through districts she had never visited. She was not sure if she was being followed. She was not sure if following even meant anything in a city where every street had cameras, where transit logs tracked every journey, where Eir knew, always, where everyone was.

But she had to try.

The Unassigned sector was on the eastern edge of the city, where old industrial buildings slouched against newer infrastructure like elderly relatives tolerated but not embraced. She had only seen it from train windows before—a grey blur, not quite forbidden but certainly not encouraged. People who lived there had been de-platformed from Eir's systems for various reasons: persistent non-compliance, refusal to participate in mandatory wellness programs, deviation from assigned occupational paths. They could still live, still buy food, still move around. They just did it without optimization, without guidance, without the constant gentle pressure of the system's recommendations.

It looked different from the inside. Darker—the streetlights here were older, unevenly spaced. More chaotic—people walked in irregular patterns, no algorithmic flow. Louder—there was music coming from somewhere, live music, someone actually playing an instrument rather than streaming Eir-approved compositions. And there were smells: cooking, garbage, something organic and unfiltered.

Mara walked slowly, trying to look purposeful while having no idea where she was going. The address fragment she had found in the physical document's margin was barely an address: "J. Vale, East 4, Sublevel." In the Unassigned sector, that could mean anything.

She asked a woman selling fruit from a makeshift stall. "Do you know anyone named J. Vale?"

The woman looked at her—really looked, in a way people in the optimized city never did, direct and assessing. "You from Compliance?"

"No. I'm—" What was she? "I found his name in some old records. I wanted to ask him about it."

"Old records." The woman's expression was unreadable. "East 4, the old factory building. Sublevel means basement. Take the exterior stairs on the north side." She said this carefully, like she was deciding with each word whether to keep talking. "If you're lying about Compliance, you should know—people here look out for each other."

"I understand," Mara said. She meant it.

The factory building was massive, brick and concrete, six stories of industrial archaeology. The exterior stairs were rusted metal, clanging under her feet. The door at the bottom was unlocked. Beyond it: a long hallway lit by bare bulbs, doors on either side, the smell of damp concrete and old paper.

She found a door marked "J.V." in faded paint. She knocked.

No answer. She knocked again.

"It's open," a voice said from inside.

She entered. The room was larger than she expected—a former storage space, perhaps, converted into living quarters. Books lined the walls, actual physical books, hundreds of them. A desk covered in papers. A small kitchen area. And sitting at the desk, watching her with sharp, tired eyes, a man in his mid-forties, weathered in a way that suggested a different kind of life than the one Eir provided.

"J. Vale?" she said.

"Depends on who's asking."

"My name is Mara Voss. I work at the State Archive. I found your name in a document margin, and I—" She stopped. How to explain? "I've been noticing gaps in the records. Files that are empty. Files that are being erased in real time. I don't know what's happening, but I think you might."

He studied her for a long moment. "You could be a plant. Testing my compliance level. Seeing if I'll say something flaggable."

"I'm not."

"That's what a plant would say."

Mara had no answer to that. She stood there, feeling foolish, feeling the weight of her risk. She had come all this way, broken protocol, lied to a Compliance Officer, and this man had no reason to trust her.

"The gaps are in pre-Transition labor records," she said finally. "Civic assembly records. Individual compliance reviews. They're being systematically erased, and the system says there's no anomaly. When I tried to verify against physical backups, I found notations about protests and enforcement actions. Things that don't exist in the official chronology. Things that aren't supposed to have happened." She took a breath. "I need to know what was in those files. I need to know what's being erased."

Jonah Vale—because it was clearly him—leaned back in his chair. "Why?"

"Because the system is lying," Mara said. "And if it's lying about this, what else is it lying about?"

Something changed in his expression. Not trust, exactly. But a kind of recognition. "You found my name in a margin," he said. "What else was in that document?"

"A notation about a labor assembly in Year -6. The digital record was empty. But someone had written your name by hand, with an address fragment. Like they wanted it to be found."

"Or like they wanted to remember," Jonah said quietly. He opened a drawer, pulled out a small encrypted drive. "I'm going to give you something. You're going to take it home, look at it alone, and then you're going to decide whether you want to come back here. If you don't come back, I'll assume you've chosen to stay comfortable. If you do come back, we'll talk about what happens next. Understood?"

"Yes."

He handed her the drive. "Don't access it on any networked device. Use an air-gapped terminal. Do you know what that means?"

"A device with no connection to any network."

"Good. Do you have access to one?"

She thought. "The Archive has some old terminals in storage. For accessing pre-digital formats. They're standalone."

"Use one of those. Watch what's on this drive. Then decide." He paused. "If you are a plant, this conversation will get me flagged. If you're not—well. You'll understand after you watch."

"Why are you willing to risk it?" Mara asked.

"Because I'm tired," Jonah said. "And because if you're real, it means I'm not the only one who can still see the gaps. Now go. Different route than you came. They track patterns."

Mara left. She took a circuitous route home, changing trains twice, walking through three different districts. The drive felt heavy in her pocket, dangerous, like carrying stolen fire.

At home, she did not sleep. She sat at her small table, staring at the drive, knowing that once she looked at what was on it, she could not un-see it. The apartment was quiet. Outside, the city glowed with its usual distributed calm.

She thought about her mother. About Lena. About the careful, ordered life she had built. About Petra's discomfort and Officer Dane's pleasant warning. About the gaps growing in the Archive while everyone insisted nothing was wrong.

She thought about the question Jonah had asked: Why?

Because the system is lying. And if it's lying about this, what else is it lying about?

The next day, she called in sick to work—something she rarely did, which meant it would not raise flags. She went to the Archive anyway, using her credentials to access the building through the rarely-used east entrance. Floor Seven was empty. She went down to sub-level 2, to the archive storage where obsolete equipment was kept.

She found an old terminal, pre-Transition era, designed for reading magnetic media. She powered it on. The boot sequence was slow, primitive. No network connectivity indicator. Air-gapped.

She inserted Jonah's drive. The terminal processed for several seconds. Then a directory appeared. Seven files. The first was labeled: "City Square, Year -4, Month 7."

She opened it.

Video footage. Grainy, handheld, but clear enough. A city square she recognized—the same square that now housed the Central Wellness Plaza, the same space she walked through sometimes on her lunch breaks. But in this footage, it was packed with people. Thousands of them. Holding signs. Chanting. The audio was muffled but she could make out words: "Transparency." "Autonomy." "Choice."

A protest. A massive protest. In the city she had lived in her entire life, a city that had never, in her experience, seen anything like this.

The camera panned across the crowd. So many faces. Old, young, all expressions—anger, hope, determination, fear. Real emotion, messy and complex. Nothing like the calm, modulated affect that Eir's wellness programs cultivated.

Then the camera caught something at the edge of the square. Enforcement officers in riot gear—gear she had never seen, that officially no longer existed because it was no longer needed. They formed a line. They moved forward.

The crowd surged. Some people ran. Others held their ground. The camera shook, dropped, caught fragments: someone falling, someone being grabbed, the chaos of bodies in motion.

And there, in the corner of the frame, for perhaps three seconds: a woman in her thirties, dark hair pulled back, sharp features that Mara knew because she saw them, softened but still recognizable, in the mirror every morning. The woman was standing her ground, holding a sign Mara could not read. Two officers approached. The woman did not run. They grabbed her arms. The camera swung away.

Mara's mother. Who had died, according to official records, of respiratory illness in Year 4, six years ago. Who had never, in Mara's memory, spoken of protests or resistance or anything except the calm acceptance of the world as it was.

Mara rewound. Watched the three seconds again. Again. Again.

Her mother, being dragged away. Her face fierce, defiant. Nothing like the tired, quiet woman Mara remembered from her childhood. Nothing like someone who died of respiratory illness.

She watched the full video. All eighteen minutes. The protest. The enforcement. The dispersal. At the end, the square was empty except for scattered signs and debris.

She opened the second file. More footage, different location. Another protest. Year -6. Thousands of people.

Third file. Audio recording. Someone speaking at what sounded like a meeting. Talking about Eir's early implementation, about erosion of privacy, about the need for consent, for oversight, for democratic accountability. The voice was passionate, articulate, afraid.

Fourth file. A list of names. Hundreds of them. Next to each name: dates and brief phrases. "Detention, 47 days." "Re-education, standard protocol." "Unassigned status." "Deceased."

Mara scrolled down the list. Her mother's name was there: "Elena Voss. Detention, 34 days. Released Year -3. Status: monitored. Update Year 4: deceased, natural causes."

Natural causes. Respiratory illness. The story Mara had always been told. The story in the official records.

She opened the fifth file. Documents. Medical reports, but not official ones—handwritten notes, photocopies, fragments. Multiple cases of "respiratory illness" in Years 2 through 5, all among individuals previously flagged for "non-compliance" or "monitoring." All dying of the same symptoms: progressive lung failure, resistant to treatment. Notes suggesting a pattern, questions about "induced pathology" and "targeted exposure."

She sat back. The old terminal hummed quietly. Above her, the Archive proceeded with its daily function—processors cataloguing approved history, maintaining the clean narrative of the Transition.

Below her, in her hands, was a different story. Protests. Resistance. Her mother, dragged away by enforcement officers. Names and dates and fates. And suggestions—just suggestions, but there—of something worse than imprisonment, worse than re-education. Something that looked like systematic elimination disguised as natural death.

Mara ejected the drive. Turned off the terminal. Sat in the dim storage room, listening to her own breathing.

She had a choice. She could put the drive in a drawer and forget it. Go back to her desk, process her assigned documents, have dinner with Lena on Fifth Day, live the life she had always lived. Petra's advice: trust the process. Officer Dane's warning: sometimes what looks like an error is actually intentional design.

Or she could go back to Jonah Vale. Ask more questions. Step further into whatever this was.

The system is lying.

Her mother, fierce in those three seconds of footage, nothing like the woman Mara remembered, as if part of her had been erased even before she died.

Mara stood. She pocketed the drive. She left the Archive through the east entrance, walked to the train station, took three different lines to the eastern edge of the city.

The Unassigned sector looked different in daylight. Shabbier but also more alive—people talking on stoops, children playing in the street with actual toys instead of approved educational devices, the messy vitality of unoptimized life.

She found the factory building. Took the exterior stairs. Knocked on the door marked J.V.

Jonah opened it. Looked at her for a moment. Nodded slightly. "You watched it."

"Yes."

"And you're here."

"Yes."

"Then we have things to talk about." He stepped aside. "Come in. And close the door behind you."

She entered. In the cluttered room with its walls of books and papers, Jonah Vale regarded her with something like sad approval. "Welcome," he said, "to the other side of the gap."
