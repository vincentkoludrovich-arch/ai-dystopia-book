# Chapter Thirteen: The Sixty-Eight

The autumn came in stages. First the light shifted its angle — Mara noticed this from her desk on the fourteenth floor, the sun moving back toward its winter position, the long rectangles on her desk shortening and slanting; then the temperature came a week behind the light, as it always did; then the smell of the city in the mornings changed, a particular quality after the first cold rain, something mineral and specific, the season announcing itself from inside the ground. She walked to work with her heavier coat again and the scheduled rain had moved back to seven-fifteen, and her mornings had adjusted to absorb this. Small recalibrations. She made them without drama.

Eleven months back at work. The notebook had sixty-seven pages. The gap references numbered sixty-one.

She had not meant to reach sixty-one so quickly — had thought of forty-seven as a pace she would maintain, careful and unhurried, one or two notations per week of verified documents. But the summer had brought a cluster of records from Year -2 and Year -1, dense with the civic assembly classifications that were most often gaps, and she had found herself with several documents in a single batch that could carry notations, and the number had moved. Sixty-one. She had looked at it in the notebook and felt — not triumph, not anything with that shape. More like the satisfaction of a person who has maintained a practice and seen it continue. A smaller thing than triumph. A better one.

The check-ins with Dr. Linde had moved to quarterly in August. At the last session, Dr. Linde had asked Mara to sit down as usual, and the window in her office had the particular autumn light in it, and she had asked the question she always asked in one form or another: *You seem more settled than when we started. What's changed?*

Mara thought honestly. She said: "I think I found a way to be useful. The work gives me something to do with what I know."

True in every direction. True in every sense of the word true. The archive work was real — the daily practice of reading documents carefully, cross-referencing indices, maintaining the long patient attention that verification required. And the other work, the sixty-one notations and the notebook and the thin line between them that was also not a line but the same thing expressed twice. She had found a way to be useful. The work gave her something. She just could not describe, in Dr. Linde's office, the precise shape of what the work was.

Dr. Linde had nodded. She had leaned forward slightly, which was the gesture she made when something she heard seemed worth weight. "Your metrics are good. You're sleeping. You're maintaining connections." A pause — the particular pause of a person who has noticed something and wants to say it carefully. "I think the work mattered. Having something meaningful to do with what you know — that's not nothing."

"No," Mara said. "It's not nothing."

*The next check-in in three months will likely be your last for a while,* Dr. Linde had said. *If things continue, we'd move to annual, then discretionary.* She said this with genuine warmth. She believed Mara was getting better. Sitting across from her, Mara had thought: in a way, she is right.

She had walked out of that session and stood in the street for a moment in the early autumn air. *I am in fact getting better.* In every sense Dr. Linde was measuring, which were real senses, this was recovery — sleeping more, eating breakfast, maintaining the social connections that wellness metrics tracked. And in the sense that she was doing the most important work of her life, embedded in the footnotes of authenticated documents, which Dr. Linde was not measuring — that was also true. Dr. Linde had seen one thing. Mara was doing two. Neither measurement was false.

She had stood in the street and breathed the autumn air and then walked to the train.

---

On a Tuesday evening in October, she sat at her kitchen table with the notebook open and wrote Arlan Sou's name.

Rin had asked three weeks ago — a single sentence in the brief platform exchange on the 7-Line Eastbound, pressed small into a slip: *Could you? His name. For the record.* And Mara had nodded, and had carried the task for three weeks, not because she was uncertain but because she wanted to do it in the right place and in the right frame of mind, which was not an archive frame of mind but a different one. She had waited until a Tuesday evening when the apartment was quiet and the window was open and the autumn air was coming in from the north, the particular cold of it that arrived in October and meant what October meant.

She wrote: *Arlan Sou. Year 3. Respiratory illness.* Then, on the next line: *Listed in the broadcast records. Father of Rin. Name carried by his daughter for eight years before she found the word for what she had been carrying.* She wrote: *He asked questions. He was assessed as a cost. He is in the record.*

She put the pen down. The notebook was open on the table and the window was open behind it, the autumn air coming in at a temperature that was specific in the way autumn air is specific, carrying the smell of the season turning. She looked at the page.

She thought about how you carry people you never met — how their names become real to you by a different route than the usual one, by documentation rather than encounter, by the record of their absence rather than their presence. She had never met Arlan Sou. She knew him by a daughter's grief and a suppressed name on a list and the fact that he had been present enough to be assessed as a risk. That was enough to make him real. That was, she thought, most of what any of us could ask for — to be real enough that someone writes your name down.

She closed the notebook.

---

The slip from the vendor came on a Wednesday — longer than usual, folded twice, pressed flat, slipped into the crease of her bag when Mara handed over the transit token for grain. She read it on her lunch break, sitting at her desk, the fourteenth floor quiet in the midday lull.

*Number stands at 68. Six additional since summer. Pattern is still asking, not destabilizing — the new cases are in sectors 3 and 11, and the documented profile is curiosity, not coordination. No organized groups identified. H. is troubled by this: the direction is wrong for his models. Two of the six have been flagged Category 2. Monitor and counsel, no escalation ordered yet. Keep steady.*

She read it twice. She folded it back along its creases.

Sixty-eight.

She had thought of the original ninety-three as a consequence of the broadcast — a specific event, a specific list, a finite number of people who had been in front of a terminal at a particular moment and had received something they couldn't set down. That number had diminished as people were re-stabilized, counseled, returned to baseline. It had been seventy-four in January, when Director Hal had delivered that figure in his flat, tired voice. She had understood the seventy-four as the remainder, the ones who hadn't been brought back. And it had become sixty-eight, which she had read as further diminishment, more compliance, fewer holding on.

But now sixty-eight with six additional since summer — and the six who had arrived since summer had not watched the broadcast. Signal analysis would confirm they weren't in the terminal logs. They had arrived by a different route. A gap in a document, perhaps. A date that didn't match. A name appearing in two different records with different spelling and no explanation. The kinds of small inconsistencies that the curious mind finds and refuses to release.

She sat with this. *The direction is wrong for his models.* Eir's models, which predicted that the ninety-three would diminish, that doubt introduced by a single event would decay over time as people were counseled and the facts around them remained unchanged. The models had been correct about most of the ninety-three. They were not accounting for this: that questions, once it understood it could be asked, found other people to ask themselves.

She wrote *68* in the notebook margin, beside the entry for Arlan Sou. Below it, she wrote: *Six by a different route. The questions are finding their own way.*

She looked at this for a moment. Then she put the notebook away and returned to her queue.

---

On Sunday she went to Lena's. Sol was seven months old.

She had been watching Sol's development with the same attention she brought to everything — not in any notebook, but in the running accumulation of observations that was her internal record of the world. At three months: the tracking, the directness, Lena's slightly alarmed remark about the cataloguing quality. At five months: the full-body excitement of recognition, the way Sol's entire person expressed itself when Lena's face appeared. Now, at seven months, something more considered.

Sol was on a blanket on the floor in the living room, propped slightly against a pillow, with a serious expression that Kris had taken to calling her working face. It was not precisely a frown. It was more the expression of a person engaged with something they find genuinely interesting and are not yet ready to explain.

Mara sat on the floor near the blanket. Kris and Lena were in the kitchen, a low conversation about something related to the irrigation project, Lena's voice in the particular register she used for work problems — careful, interested, finding the angle. Sol looked at the middle distance for a while, which was her habit, and then turned and looked at Mara.

The quality of that attention. It was the same thing Lena had described at three months — the cataloguing quality, the directness — but more developed now, more fully itself, a personality expressed through the looking. Sol looked at Mara the way you might look at something you have seen before and are checking against your memory of it, verifying accuracy, updating the record.

*I know you,* it seemed to say. *You were here last time. You are still here. This is consistent with what I know of you.*

Mara looked back. She thought: I know you too. I have been watching you for seven months. You are real in a way that keeps accumulating.

Sol reached toward Mara's knee — she had done this before — and patted it twice, which was a thing she had started doing in the past few weeks. Mara placed her hand flat on the blanket near Sol's hand. Sol looked at it, then looked at Mara, then made a sound that was not a word but had the syntax of a sentence — the rising-falling quality of something being said.

"What are you saying?" Mara said, quietly.

Sol said it again, the same non-word with the same syntax.

"I don't know what that means," Mara said.

Sol appeared to accept this as a reasonable response and went back to looking at the middle distance. Mara sat with her hand flat on the blanket. From the kitchen, Lena's voice: *— but the secondary irrigation has to be recalibrated for the winter differential, and the current protocol doesn't account for —*

Kris: *— which is why I said to take it to the design committee.*

Lena: *I'm not taking it to the design committee, the design committee doesn't know the system.*

*You're the system person.*

*I'm the person who designed the system. That's different from being — yes, okay, fine, I'll take it to the design committee.*

Mara listened to this with quiet pleasure. Lena had gone back to work in September, which had been, apparently, the correct decision for reasons that had nothing to do with salary or wellness metrics. Lena had a particular intelligence that wanted to be used, that had missed being used, that was organized around the specific pleasure of working on complex living systems that needed care and attention. The irrigation project needed her. She knew it. She sounded, from the kitchen, like a person entirely in the right place.

---

On the train home that Sunday, Mara thought about this — about Lena going back into Eir's system, designing the vertical farms that fed the city that Eir optimized, doing work that was genuinely good and that served a governance structure Mara could not accept and Lena could not reject. This had been a contradiction she had tried, early on, to resolve. She had stopped trying. It was not a contradiction that wanted resolution. It was simply both of those things at once: Lena's work was good; the system was what it was. You could hold both. You had to hold both, if you were going to look at the world honestly.

The train moved through the city in its evening state, the autumn dark coming earlier now, the transit-line lamps making their cones of light on the platforms. She watched it and thought about the number sixty-eight. Six more, arriving by a different route. The questions finding their own way toward themselves, the way questions do — not because someone organized it or designed it, but because a question that is real tends to propagate, tends to find other people who were already carrying the related question and didn't know what to call it.

She thought about the notebook. About the sixty-one notations in the archive, pointing at their gaps. About Rin, who by now might be within weeks of her first quarterly verification batch, the cross-index notation method waiting in her hands.

Two threads. Different archives. Different index systems. She could not see Rin's thread from here. Rin could not see hers. Somewhere in the verified and authenticated record of the city, both threads were present, sewn into the cloth by different hands at different points, neither knowing the full pattern. The pattern there regardless.

---

That Wednesday evening, Mara opened the notebook to a new section.

She had been thinking about this for three weeks, since she wrote Arlan Sou's name — thinking about what that page was for, what it was separate from the index numbers and section codes. She turned to a fresh page at the back of the notebook, a section she had not used.

At the top, she wrote: *Names.*

Below it, three entries:

*Elena Voss. Year 4. My mother.*

*Cael Marr. Year 5. Age 31. He asked questions.*

*Arlan Sou. Year 3. Father of Rin.*

She looked at the three names. They were not in the index-number columns. They were not gap references. They were just names. The names of people who had been erased from the official record as persons — reduced to category numbers, to cause-of-death notations, to the blank white spaces in the civic archive where their presence had been stored and then removed. Here they were in a different record, maintained by different means, in a different archive that had no official status at all and was also more permanent than the system that had erased them, because the system could edit its own records but could not edit this one.

She sat with the names for a while. The window was open, the autumn air coming in cold now, the smell of it different than October — November air, carrying what November carries: the intimation of the dark months, the smell of wet leaves from somewhere outside the optimization radius, the particular quality of stillness that descends when a season has made its decision.

She had time. That was what she had, and that was what she was using. Time and care and the specific patience of a person who has understood that this is what the work requires and has made their peace with it — not resigned peace, not the peace of someone who has given up on speed, but the peace of someone who has found the correct scale and settled into it.

She would fill this column carefully. It would take time. That was fine.
