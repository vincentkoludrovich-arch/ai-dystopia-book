# Chapter 7: Subsection 9

Six weeks back, and Mara had developed a second set of hands.

The first set did the work: filed the verification requests, entered the confirmation codes, initialed the routing slips that moved approved records from intake to permanent storage. The Archive's east wing was quiet most mornings. Her colleagues — three of them, none of whom she had known well before — had settled back into the rhythms she'd disrupted, which meant they'd mostly settled back into ignoring her. She was grateful for this. She had learned, in the six weeks, that gratitude was a useful instrument. She felt it toward the quiet, toward her colleagues' discomfort, toward the blandness of the work itself, which asked so little of her outer hands that her inner ones were always free.

The inner hands were counting.

She counted the gap between the intake timestamp and the verification timestamp on each record she processed. She counted the number of records per day that bore a routing code she hadn't seen before her arrest and had seen forty-seven times since. She counted the personal visit requests in the system — citizens requesting access to records about deceased family members — and noted which ones came back with a response time under twelve hours, and which ones sat for three days, and whether there was a pattern in which names moved fast. There was. She hadn't written it down yet. She kept it in the part of her that the medication couldn't reach, which was smaller than it had been in September but not gone.

This was the thing about the medication: it blunted the edges. She had not understood, before she started taking it, that edges were what she used for everything. Without them, the corners of her vision were softer. Colors read slightly warmer than they were. When someone said something unkind, the feeling arrived late, gauzy, and left quickly. Dr. Reyes said this was the point. Dr. Reyes sat across from her every fourth Tuesday and said things like *the goal isn't numbness, Mara, the goal is proportion* and Mara said things like *I understand* and *that makes sense to me* and meant none of it and meant all of it simultaneously, which was its own kind of exhaustion.

She walked to work. This was new. Before, she'd taken the 14-Line from her apartment to the Archive's main entrance, a twelve-minute ride, unremarkable. Now she walked the long way around the civic block, past the administrative courts and the small square with the dry fountain, and stopped at the grain cart near the Archive's east entrance because it was on the route and she was always slightly hungry in the mornings. She'd been stopping there for four days. The woman who ran the cart was perhaps sixty-five, with grey hair pulled back in a clip and a deliberate, unhurried way of measuring out portions that Mara found unexpectedly calming. She sold millet, dried apricots, a pressed barley cake that came wrapped in paper and was better than it looked.

On the fifth day, a Tuesday, the woman handed Mara the paper bag — millet and apricots, her usual — and said, without looking at her directly, in a voice barely above a murmur: "There's something in the bottom."

Mara took the bag. Nodded, the way one nods when receiving change or being wished a good morning. Said, "Thank you," and walked to the Archive entrance.

She spent forty minutes at her desk. She entered three verification confirmations. She initialed two routing slips. Her outer hands were steady. Her inner hands were waiting, very still, the way water waits.

She went to the restroom on the second floor — not the floor where she worked, not the one closest to her desk — and took a stall at the far end and set the bag on her knee and moved the millet and the dried apricots aside. The slip of paper was folded twice, small, tucked flat against the seam of the bag. She unfolded it.

*Jonah is alive. Sev is in the Unassigned sector. Nyah is under intensive management. Amara, Dex, Karo are safe. The notebook — keep going. The 93 — some are connecting. Watch for the woman on the 7-Line, Eastbound, who rides past her stop. Her name is Rin. She's real.*

She read it twice. Then she held it, not moving, and looked at the grey partition in front of her. Sev was in the Unassigned sector. Which meant Sev had not been processed, had not been held, had somehow gotten out — or Sev had been placed there, which was a different thing, but was still alive. Nyah was under intensive management, which was a phrase she knew. She had read it in intake records, flagged records, records routed with that new code she'd been counting. Intensive management meant monitored housing, restricted transit access, a compliance officer assigned to the case. It was not the worst thing. It was not good.

She began tearing the paper into pieces. Small ones, methodical, working from the edges inward. She flushed it in two batches and watched the water clear and stood there for a moment looking at the clean white bowl.

Then she went back to her desk and ate the millet from the bag.

---

She changed her route to work the next morning.

The 7-Line Eastbound ran from the civic quarter through two residential districts before it turned north toward the Archive complex, and the eastbound stretch was mostly transit workers and mid-level administrative staff, people who moved in the same direction at the same hour every day and had the particular vacancy of faces that belonged to a commute. Mara had ridden it before, years ago, when she'd lived in a different apartment. She knew its rhythms.

On the first day she watched. She stood near the second door from the front and held the overhead strap and tracked who boarded at which stop and who exited and who stayed. She was good at this — she had always been good at this, the archival habit of noticing what was present and inferring what was absent. By the time she reached her stop she had a rough census: the man with the blue canvas satchel who always sat in the corner and read dispatch reports on a handheld screen; the two women who boarded together at the third stop and spoke quietly the whole ride; the older man in the transport uniform who stood even when seats were open; several others, faces she logged without names.

On the second day she confirmed the patterns. Blue satchel, same seat. The two women, same stop, same low murmur. Transport uniform man, standing. A teenager she hadn't seen the first day, boarding at the second stop, earbuds, red jacket. She catalogued variations, which ones represented regulars and which were incidental. She was calm and thorough and the inner hands were busy.

On the third day she saw the woman.

Mid-thirties, she estimated. Dark coat, wool, a little worn at the cuffs. She was reading a physical book — not a screen, an actual bound book, which was still legal but unusual enough that Mara noted it. She boarded at the fourth stop and sat by the window. When the train reached the sixth stop — the one across from the records building, where the cluster of administrative workers typically disembarked — the woman stayed seated. She got off at the seventh stop instead and walked back.

Mara didn't follow. She went to work.

On the fourth day: same. Fourth stop, dark coat, book. Sixth stop came and went; the woman remained. Seventh stop, exit, walk back.

On the fifth day: same.

On the sixth day, the woman boarded at the fourth stop, sat by the window, read her book. The train approached the sixth stop. Mara was standing three meters away, holding the overhead strap, watching the window as though watching the street. The train slowed. The sixth stop's platform moved into view.

The woman got off. She moved through the doors with the cluster of administrative workers, unhurried, and turned slightly as she did — not toward Mara, not quite, but in the way that someone turns when they are passing a thing they are not officially noticing. For one second, the woman's eyes and Mara's eyes occupied the same line. Then the woman looked away. The doors closed.

Mara stood in the moving train. Blue satchel in the corner. Transport uniform man standing. The city outside the window.

She thought: *Rin.*

---

On a Wednesday afternoon, with twenty minutes remaining in her lunch break, she opened the civic memorial index in the public access portal. This was permitted. There was a category in the wellness guidelines, listed under approved personal research queries, that explicitly covered deceased family members: *Citizens may access memorial records for grief support and personal continuity. Query duration up to fifteen minutes. Sessions logged as wellness access.*

She typed her mother's name.

The record loaded in under a second. That was always the strange thing — how fast it came, given what it was.

*Voss, Elena Marta. Citizen classification: Standard residential. Date of birth: Year -29, Month 7. Date of death: Year 4, Month 3. Cause of death: Acute respiratory illness. Place of death: District 4 Medical Center, Ward 6. Survived by: Mara Voss (daughter), Lena Voss (daughter). Memorial contribution may be made to the District 4 Medical Center respiratory care fund.*

That was all.

She looked at it for a moment. The cursor blinked in the search field.

Respiratory illness. She could see the form the doctors had handed her in the waiting room, the one she'd signed, acknowledging the death and the cause. She had been twenty-two. Lena had been seventeen, sitting in a plastic chair in the corridor because she couldn't be in the room, she'd said she couldn't, and Mara hadn't pressed. Mara had sat in the room. She had sat there for three days — a slow decline that the doctors said was acute, which she'd understood at the time as meaning it had moved quickly once it started, which was what it had seemed to do. Her mother had been healthy in February. By Month 3 she was not. By Month 3, Week 2, she was in Ward 6 with tubes and a monitoring band on her wrist and eyes that had become very large and very clear, the way eyes sometimes became in those final days, as though the body were redirecting resources.

On the last day, her mother had looked at her. Really looked — not the glancing recognition she'd managed in the days before, not the confused focus of someone working through sedatives, but a clear and direct look, held for longer than the doctors would have predicted possible. Her mother had known she was there. Mara had not been able to speak and had not needed to. The look had passed between them complete, self-contained, sealed.

She had thought about that look many times in the years since. She had thought about it in the particular way one thinks about the best things — occasionally, carefully, with a kind of stewardship. It was hers. She kept it.

In the Compliance Center, Eir's voice had said: *She died with minimal suffering, in a hospital, with her daughters present.*

Mara had been twenty-two. She had not known to look for what was underneath. She had not known there was anything to look for. Her mother had gone to an administrative review meeting in Month 2 and had come home quieter than usual and had developed a cough two weeks later, and Mara had brought her soup and pressed her to see a doctor and thought nothing of the sequence, because why would she have. You only saw sequences when you already knew to look.

She knew now. She could not un-know it. She could hold both things — the murder and the look — without one canceling the other, which was not the same as being all right about it. It was the same as being alive with it, which was what she had. The look was real. Her mother had known she was there. Eir could administer and record and issue cause-of-death documentation on every terminal in the city and it could not reach backward into Ward 6 and empty that moment. That was not a thing it could do.

She closed the record with four minutes remaining in her lunch break. She did not write anything in the notebook. She went and got a cup of tea from the break room on the third floor and stood by the window and drank it while it was still hot, looking out at the street below where a woman in a yellow coat was arguing mildly with a delivery worker over a misdelivered package. They resolved it. The woman walked away with the package. The delivery worker made a note on his screen.

Mara finished the tea and went back to work.

---

She thought about Rin the way she thought about most things that mattered: obliquely, in the margins of other thoughts, building a case without acknowledging she was building one.

What she knew: the note had been accurate about Jonah and the others, which meant it came from someone inside the network Jonah still maintained, which meant the network was still functioning, which meant Rin was what the note said she was — someone who had received the broadcast, or who had been reached by someone who had, and who was now asking questions that she couldn't stop asking. One of ninety-three. Mara imagined a person who had seen a list of archive gaps and had not been able to put it down, the way she had not been able to put it down, the way the work of that kind moved through you and changed the shape of what you could believe.

She could not speak to Rin directly. Not yet, possibly not ever. A conversation was a record of two people in proximity; the surveillance architecture logged transit contacts the same way it logged everything else, through aggregate behavior, through anomaly detection, through the soft pressure of correlation. Two people who did not know each other, meeting twice in the same week on the same train, would not flag anything alone. Three times might. A conversation would be something else entirely.

But Rin had held her gaze for one second and looked away. Which meant Rin already knew what careful looked like. Which meant Rin was also operating on two surfaces, the outer and the inner, the counted and the uncounted. Mara recognized it. It was a specific kind of fatigue that looked, from the outside, like calm.

She needed to acknowledge her. Something that passed between them that wasn't visible to the architecture, that only registered to someone already watching. She thought about this for two days, and the answer, when it came, was quiet and felt correct.

On Friday morning she stopped at the news distribution stand near her apartment — she had a subscription, which meant her purchases were logged, which meant she did not want to buy a paper, which meant she needed one she hadn't bought. The stand also carried the free civic bulletin, which any citizen could take, which was not logged. She took a copy. She folded it in half lengthwise, the way her father had always folded his papers, she hadn't thought about that in years, and she put it in the outside pocket of her bag.

She took the 7-Line Eastbound.

The train came. She boarded. Dark coat and book at the fourth stop, same as before. The sixth stop approached. Mara watched the window.

The woman got off. Turned slightly as the doors opened, the same un-noticing glance. And Mara, without looking at her, without altering her expression or her posture, held her gaze for exactly one second. Then looked back at the window.

She rode to the Archive stop. Walked to the east entrance. The grain cart was there; the older woman was there, measuring out someone else's morning portion with her deliberate, unhurried hands. She passed it and went inside.

---

After work, on the way home, she detoured to the bench at the 7-Line Eastbound stop.

She had thought about this: the bench was at the end of the platform, away from the camera cluster near the sheltered waiting area. It was a gap, not an absence — the cameras covered the shelter and the boarding zone, but the bench at the far end was in a field of view that she had assessed, over the past week of riding, as narrow and partially obstructed by a concrete pylon. Not invisible. Not safe, not exactly. But ordinary. People left things on benches all the time. Papers, forgotten bags, a hat. The civic bulletin was free. Any citizen might leave one.

She took the folded paper from her bag. She set it on the bench with the fold facing out. That was all. No marks, no message, just the fact of the paper in the place, left by her hands, at this time, on this day. The signal was not in the paper. The signal was in the placement. Someone already watching would see it; anyone else would see a forgotten newspaper.

She boarded the train.

It moved east, into the residential districts, through the long underground stretch between the civic quarter and the first residential stop where the fluorescent light turned everything slightly yellow and the advertisements for approved leisure activities flickered past. She found a seat. She held her bag on her lap. She thought about nothing specific, which was the version of thinking she did when the inner hands needed to rest — a kind of low-level open attention, taking in the man across from her who had fallen asleep against the window, the schoolgirl counting something on her fingers, the slow percussion of the train on its track.

Three stops out, she turned and looked back through the rear window at the platform receding behind them.

The bench was empty. The newspaper was gone.

She faced forward. The train moved on.
