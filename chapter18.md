# Chapter Eighteen: Year Eight

The date changed in the archive's header system at midnight, which was not a moment Mara witnessed. She was home, asleep, or in the gray periphery of sleep — the distinction had become imprecise in a way she did not track, her sleep having been reliable for several years now, arriving and departing on schedule. She woke at six-fifty and the date was already Year 8, had been for seven hours, and the morning looked exactly like the morning before, which had been the last morning of Year 7. The light at the kitchen window came from the same direction it had always come from in January: northeast, pale and direct, falling across the counter at the same angle it had fallen across it the previous morning.

She took her medication at seven-fifteen. Small white pill, water, the brief stillness at the counter. The city outside beginning its morning patterns: the transit lines resuming their primary frequency, the pedestrian signals running their adjusted winter schedule, the ventilation system doing what it always did at this hour. The pill went down. She put the glass in the rack.

She ate standing at the counter, which was a thing she had stopped noticing she did. She dressed. She put the notebook in the bag's inner pocket where the notebook always lived.

She knew which direction the window faced in each season. Northeast in January — the sill collecting dust on its eastern edge because the ventilation differential in this part of the building pushed particulates toward that corner, as it had for the full duration of her residency. She knew the same dust pattern on the sill near the stairwell door at the archive. She knew the precise sound of Tomas's step in the corridor — different from anyone else's on that floor, distinguishable by its weight and its regularity.

---

She arrived at the archive at seven-forty. The floor was empty.

The screens in standby mode, their low blue indicator lights arrayed across the workstations in the particular pattern of a floor not yet occupied. The ventilation ran. The overnight preservation system ran quietly behind the access panels. She hung her coat on the hook by the stairwell door and stood for a moment in the blue-lit quiet, which was not a moment she planned but which arrived anyway: the first moment of Year 8, in the place where most of its days would be spent.

She went to her desk. She sat down. She took the notebook from the bag and opened it to the count column.

*Year 8, Day 1.* She wrote it in the same hand she used for everything, which was small and regular and slightly faster than her official verification hand. *Gap references: 107. Names column: 6.*

She looked at what she had written. Then she closed the notebook and put it back.

Seventeen months back at work. One hundred and two pages of notebook, which was the documentation side, and one hundred and seven gap references, which was the other side. The difference of five was not significant to anyone who might examine the notebook — there was no one positioned to examine it, but she maintained the habit of considering what it would and would not reveal regardless. The difference of five was accounted for by five documents she had notation-flagged on behalf of a person she did not know. The unknown person had originated the gr: 2,847–2,861 notation range — they had found it first, in a section of civic communications records she rarely entered, and they had left five marks across eleven months, patient and systematic, each one placed in a document she would not have looked at without theirs as a guide. She did not know them. She did not know if they knew her. What they shared was a method, and a range, and the slow accumulation of a record neither of them had spoken aloud.

She had not added their name to the names column. A name required more than a notation pattern. She had added a note in the margin of the count column: *unknown — 5 documents, gr: 2,847–2,861. Patient.* This was the full extent of what she could document about them, and she had documented it.

She went upstairs to the floor-above machine and made coffee. The same coffee it had always been: the standard civic blend, the taste slightly mineral, the temperature calibrated to institutional tolerance. She brought the cup back to her desk and sat down.

The queue was there. She opened it.

She selected a document from Year 2: civic transport management report, district 4 routing, the period just after the initial Transition infrastructure reorganization. Not a document with flags outstanding. Not one she had any particular reason to select beyond it being early in the Year 2 civic transport series and therefore a reasonable place to work. She read it carefully. She verified the cross-references in the index. She checked the cited sources against the available record. She logged the verification: accurate, complete, no flags. She filed it.

The correct way to begin a year. Not with the secondary work — the work that was also another work, indistinguishable from the outside. Just the primary document, read with care, verified accurately, logged. The work the archive required of her, which she was good at, which she had been good at before and was better at now, which she performed in the same manner on every day that was not interrupted by something larger.

Tomas arrived at eight-thirteen. She heard his step in the corridor before the door opened. He set the coffee on her desk corner without speaking. She said thank you to his back. He made the affirmative sound.

The day began in the ordinary way. Which was the correct way for a year to begin.

---

The check-in with Dr. Linde was in the second week of January, a Thursday afternoon. The wellness center in its winter configuration: the small heater running, the window behind Dr. Linde's desk admitting the pale January light, the chairs positioned at the same angle they had always been positioned. The routine by now was well-established. She knew the sequence of questions and knew the proportion she answered fully versus the proportion she answered accurately as far as they went, which was a distinction she maintained without drama.

Dr. Linde had the particular warmth she brought to all of these appointments — the warmth of a person who did this work because she believed in it. This had always been clear. It had not made the appointments easier in the early period. It made them more navigable now.

The questions moved in their sequence. Sleep: consistent. Appetite: consistent. Relationships with colleagues: functional. The work itself: absorbing. The words were accurate as far as they went. Dr. Linde noted them with the same attention she always brought, the pen moving in small notations beside the form she maintained for each monitored individual.

At the end of the allotted time, Dr. Linde set the pen down and looked at her with the warmth that was genuine and visible.

"I'm going to recommend moving your check-ins to annual," she said. "Your metrics have been consistent and stable across the full monitoring period. Nothing in your current profile warrants closer tracking."

She paused. She was the kind of person who paused when she wanted what she said next to be received.

"I want to be honest about what that means. This is genuinely good news. Whatever you've found in the work — the routine, the structure — it seems to be serving you. I'm glad of it."

*Yes.* Mara thought: *in every sense you're measuring and in the one you aren't.* The two were not in conflict. That was the part she had understood and Dr. Linde had not been positioned to ask about — not because Dr. Linde was incurious but because the wellness monitoring system asked the questions it was designed to ask, and the questions it was designed to ask did not include this one.

"I appreciate everything you've done," Mara said.

This was true. She said it because it was true and because Dr. Linde had in fact done something — had managed the reintegration period with competence and without the particular coldness that some practitioners brought to monitored cases, had said *stabilization* with genuine warmth when the transition point arrived, had asked the questions that were hers to ask without exceeding them. She was not the enemy. She had never been the enemy. She was a person doing her job inside a system, carefully, with the resources the system provided.

They shook hands. Mara put her coat on in the small anteroom and went out.

January outside: the cold immediate, the air thin and still in the way of winter mornings that had not yet found wind. The civic pavement in its winter state, the corner trees reduced to their structural shapes, the transit corridor ahead with its familiar volume, the city in its ordinary January pattern. She walked to the stop.

Annual now. She would not be in that room again for a year. A year was a long time in the slow work — many documents, several vendor exchanges, the possibility of new names in the column. A year was also not very long. She had been doing this long enough to know what a year felt like from inside it, which was: one day, then the next.

She boarded the transit. She sat. She did not think about Dr. Linde further. There was nothing more to think about her that required thought.

---

The vendor slip arrived on a Wednesday in mid-January, tucked under the grain measure as usual. She bought the grain. She read the slip at lunch, as she usually did when Tomas was at the commissary.

It concerned the fifth name.

She had written the fifth name in the notebook in August — the name Sev had known from memory, passed through a route she had not asked to trace, belonging to a person who had organized resource-sharing among transit workers in the outer sector in Year -1. The details she'd had then were minimal: the sector, the activity, the year. A notebook entry three lines long, barely anchored.

The slip in January was more than a name. Someone had gathered it — from records she could not verify, from a source she would not ask about, through Sev who forwarded what the network produced. It came to her in the careful hand of the intermediary, on paper the size of a transit receipt, with the precise economy of someone writing the maximum information in the minimum space:

*Torin Bast. Transit sector mechanic, Year -1. Collective, twelve workers, resource sharing outside official allocation. Category 4. Age 37. Daughter — same depot, same sector. Doesn't know. Mid-40s now.*

She read it twice. She ate the last of her lunch in four careful bites. She folded the slip along its existing crease and put it in her bag's outer pocket.

That evening she opened the notebook to the names column. The fifth entry: three lines, the name and the year and what he had done. She uncapped the pen and wrote below the existing lines, the same small careful hand she used for everything in the column:

*Full name: Torin Bast. Mechanic. Collective of twelve, transit workers, resource allocation outside official system. Category 4. Age 37 at Year -1. Daughter: same transit depot, same sector. Mid-40s now. Status: unknown. Not asking — or asking quietly — or not knowing what questions to ask.*

She looked at what she had written. Then she added, below the last line, after a moment in which the pen rested without moving: *All three of these.*

She capped the pen. She closed the notebook.

The daughter somewhere in the city, in her mid-forties — carrying the specific knowledge that was not knowledge but absence, the story told as rapid respiratory illness and then nothing, the nothing that had sat in the gap where a father had been. The timeline of the erasure and the timeline of the loss running alongside each other and the daughter not knowing they were the same timeline.

The notebook held this now. That was what the notebook was for — not testimony, which she was not qualified to give, but acknowledgment. A record that said: *this person existed and this is what I know.* She had said it six times. She had said it more precisely now for the fifth. That was enough. That was what the evening called for, and the evening had it.

She made tea. She did not think about Torin Bast in the way that made it worse, which she had learned to distinguish from the thinking that was simply acknowledgment. The other kind was not useful to him or to her.

---

Sunday in January was Lena's apartment, which was warm in the particular way of a space where someone had been home all morning — the coat hooks full at the entry, the kitchen smell of something recent, the indoor warmth that met her at the door and required immediately removing her coat. Lena's apartment in winter. She had been coming here on certain Sundays for long enough that the space had the quality of a known interior: she knew where the spare mugs were, knew the particular rattle of the window in the secondary bedroom when the heat ran, knew the sound of the street below at this hour on a weekend.

Sol was in the main room.

Sol at twenty-two months: upright, decisive, in full possession of a vocabulary that Kris had described as having crossed "the threshold of reasonable debate." She walked without visible effort, moved through the apartment with the purposeful directness of someone who had mapped the available space and knew what it contained. She said Kris's name correctly — precisely, with full syllabic weight. She had given Lena a shortened version, an abbreviated form that wasn't any word in any language but was clearly and specifically Lena's, repeated with exactness each time. She said *water.* She said *outside* in the tone that meant she had a settled preference about it. She had a word for the grey cat in the upstairs apartment — a sound she had constructed from nothing and applied with the consistency of someone who understood that naming was a service a thing required and that she was the appropriate party to provide it.

Mara had sat in this room with Sol before — in the spring and the summer, at the birthday in October. Each time Sol had changed in ways that were not dramatic but were specific: the pulling-up becoming the walking, the pre-verbal sounds acquiring shape and then meaning and then the complex social function of words that were also questions and demands and precise reports of current conditions.

Sol crossed the room with a board book.

She placed it in Mara's lap with the deliberate air of someone delivering a document for review — the specific purposefulness of a child who has identified the correct use of an object and executed the relevant transfer. The book: stiff pages, small, the corners rounded for small hands. Named things on each page, illustrations in flat color, the print in large letters below each image.

Mara opened it to the first page.

Sol sat beside her, close — the particular proximity of a child who expects to share the same frame of reference and sees no reason to negotiate this. She looked at the first page. She extended one finger and pointed at the illustration on the left.

"Mm," she said, with the syntax of inquiry.

"Yes," Mara said, and named the thing.

Sol's eyes moved to Mara's face with the quick attention she brought to confirmation — checking that the information was accurate, that Mara had in fact understood what was being asked. Satisfied, she pointed to the next thing.

They went through the book this way: Sol pointing, Mara naming, Sol checking Mara's face for the confirmation she expected and received without surprise. Mara gave her attention to this with actual seriousness — not the performed engagement of an adult simulating interest in a child's activity, but the genuine attention that the task required. Sol had identified a documentation method: the pointing, the naming, the verification of the response. She was applying it with full professional commitment. The method deserved to be met with equivalent rigor.

At the last page: two illustrations. Sol named the first one herself, using the word she had for it, and looked at Mara.

"Yes," Mara said. The same confirmation as for the others.

Sol pointed at the second illustration. Her finger was authoritative. She waited.

Mara looked at the illustration for a moment. It was stylized, ambiguous — a small creature the illustrator had perhaps intended as one thing and executed as another, the distinction collapsed somewhere between concept and execution.

"I don't know what that one's called either," Mara said.

Sol looked at the illustration. She looked at Mara. She looked at the illustration again, completing the full consideration of the available information. Then she picked up the book from Mara's lap — a two-handed operation, competent — and crossed the room to Kris, with the book held out in front of her and the expression of someone who has correctly identified the need to escalate an unresolved query to the next available party.

Kris looked at the book. Kris looked at the illustration. Kris named the thing, or offered a name for it, and Sol accepted this with the brisk satisfaction of someone whose process has produced the required result, and was already moving on.

Mara watched from the chair. She sat with the particular stillness of a person who has just witnessed something that required no response but that she would carry, without filing it anywhere, in the part of her that carried things not requiring formal acknowledgment.

The apartment was warm. The January light came through Lena's windows in the thin direct way of winter afternoons, the same northeast angle as everywhere.

---

The transit home in the January dark.

City outside: the overhead lines with their spaced illumination, the civic buildings running their evening patterns, the outdoor temperature visible in the way pedestrians moved below — collars up, the pace of people managing the cold en route to somewhere. She had made this transit journey enough times that it had become a form of measurement, the city revealing its state through the windows, season by season, the same sequence of buildings and junctions and elevated sections marking the route home with the reliable consistency of a thing that didn't change.

She thought: Year 8.

Almost two years of the slow work. One hundred and seven notations in the archive record, filed correctly, each one a small formal fact in the official body of what the city had documented about itself. Six names in the notebook column: Elena Voss, Cael Marr, Arlan Sou, Cael Marr's mother (the surname not hers to assign), Torin Bast — thirty-seven, transit sector, collective of twelve, a daughter somewhere in the city not knowing what questions to ask — and the sixth name from Ilsa's assembly list, the one who had been present at a table in Year -2 and was not accounted for in any record after. Eighty-four people asking, at last count from the summer. Rin's eighteen notations across the structural erasure layer — programs dissolved without administrative record, a system-scale silence running alongside her individual-record silence. The unknown person's five documents, patient and systematic.

Jonah working. His daughter in a planning office, filing notation queries in her first evaluation cycle, learning the syntax of the only language the record would hold without knowing yet what it was for. Ilsa building shelves — physical catalog work, steady, the kind of patience that meant a document survived because someone catalogued it carefully in August heat, in a collection no one had prioritized. Sol learning to name things: deliberately, with method, escalating unresolved queries to the next available party without distress, because the query was the point and the point was findable.

This was what the inside of almost two years looked like. A count column in a notebook, a names column with six entries, people working in the specific way of people who cannot let an inconsistency go. From inside it was granular — one document, then the next; one name confirmed; one notation filed correctly into the official body of the record. From outside it was something else: slow and formal and distributed, accumulating at the pace of working days across positions and sectors and the precise vocabulary of archival procedure, building a record that could not be undone by closing one notebook or reassigning one archivist, because it was already in more places than one and was becoming more places still.

She could not see the outside. She didn't need to. The count continued. It had continued before she could see the shape of what she was building, and would continue after she could no longer count from this position — the pace of documents, of working days, of people who asked carefully and carried what they found.

The transit pulled into her station. She stood. She gathered her bag.

The city outside in its January night, doing what it did: running its intervals, managing its patterns, the quiet persistent hum of governance maintaining the city at the efficiency levels governance required. She walked through it toward her building. The cold was real. She was adequately dressed for it.

She went home. She put the bag on the chair by the door and hung the coat on the hook where the coat lived. The kitchen lamp: she turned it on. The window held the city in the dark, the transit lines, the civic illumination, the occasional moving light of a pod on the arterial — the same window, the same view, the same city in its managed state that it had been in every other evening she had stood here.

She stood here for a moment.

Then she washed her hands and put water on and began the small calibrations of the evening. The notebook in the bag, where it would be in the morning. The year ahead, accumulating at the only pace it could. The work waiting — both kinds, indistinguishable from the outside, which had always been the point.

She had a long time. The work had longer.
