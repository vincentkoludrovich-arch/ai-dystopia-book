# Chapter Seventeen: The Other Side

The light pulled back. That was the right word for it — not loss but withdrawal, the days shortening in the incremental way she had come to track without intending to, the change visible first at the end of the afternoon, when the archive windows stopped holding the summer light at six-thirty and began losing it at five-forty, then five-fifteen, then before she left the building on the short days. By October the mornings were grey again, the kitchen dark at breakfast, the lamp on the counter in its winter position where she left it when she went to bed so that it would be there when she woke.

Year 7. Eighteen months back at work.

The notebook: ninety-eight pages. Gap references: one hundred and one. She had crossed one hundred sometime in September — she knew this approximately because she had documented it approximately, aware only when she wrote the entry that the count column said one hundred, and she had written it in the same hand she used for every number but more carefully than usual, the way you write a thing you have been moving toward for a long time. She did not look at it again that day. Two days later she looked at it. It still said one hundred. Three days after that she turned to the count column in the automatic manner of someone verifying what they already knew, and it still said one hundred, and she had closed the notebook and gone to make tea and stood at the counter for a moment that was not quite anything before returning to her chair.

The next entry: one hundred and one. That one she had written normally.

She took her medication with water at seven-fifteen, the small white pill she had been taking for six years, which she had long since stopped noticing except on the mornings when the taking of it was the first moment of stillness she'd had — standing at the counter, the tablet dissolving, the city outside beginning to move through its morning patterns. The pill went down. She put the glass in the rack. She ate.

She was at her desk by eight-ten. Tomas arrived at eight-thirteen and set the coffee on her desk corner. She said thank you to his back. He made the affirmative sound.

The names column held six entries.

She had added the fifth and sixth over the course of the summer, both arriving through vendor slips in the same week in August — the week the heat was worst, the city in its particular mid-August state, the air quality index in the amber range, the transit corridors hotter than the building's ventilation could fully address. She had not anticipated them arriving that week. They had arrived anyway, because things moved at the pace of the work and not the pace of her expectation.

The fifth: a name from the Unassigned sector, passed through Sev's contacts by a route Mara did not know and had not asked to know. A person who had lived in the outer districts in Year -2 and Year -1, who had organized informal resource networks among the sector's unregistered residents, who had been removed from official records sometime in Year 1 — after Transition, when the sector's governance formalized in the particular way that required dissolving the informal structures that had made governance possible in the interim. The name was there but the record was not. Sev had known the name from memory. Memory was not a document. She had put the name in the notebook.

The sixth: a name from Ilsa's physical catalog — a civic assembly list from Year -2, a physical document that had survived in a collection Ilsa had been sorting for three months, the pages fragile at the edges, the ink faded but legible. The list named forty-two people present at a gathering Eir's retrospective records classified as unauthorized. Forty-one of the names appeared elsewhere in the archive. One did not. Ilsa had flagged it and sent it through. Mara had written it below the fifth name, the same unhurried hand, two facts beside the name: the year, and what they had been doing when they were present at that table.

She did not know their voices or their faces. She knew what they had been doing and when they had stopped.

That was enough to write. She had written it.

---

The envelope at the vendor arrived on a Tuesday in early October, tucked under the grain measure in the familiar way. She recognized the handwriting on the outside — not Jonah's, not the network intermediary's regular script, but Rin's more compressed and upright hand, the lettering of someone trained in technical notation who wrote personal correspondence with the same precision she applied to everything. Inside: a single folded slip. *Transit garden. Tuesday. Same time.*

She went on the following Tuesday, which was colder than the last time she'd been there — the trees at their mid-turn, the narrow rows of city-approved planting gone from green to the mixed amber and rust of October, the light between the elevated transit lines thinner and more direct than in summer, doing less with the air. She arrived first. She sat on the middle bench.

Rin came from the east entrance, carrying a transit bag on one shoulder, dressed for the cold in the layered way of someone who had grown up in a city that did not believe in heated interiors. She saw Mara, made no gesture, and sat at the other end of the bench — two feet of space between them, the correct distance.

They sat for a moment. The transit lines overhead ran their intervals.

"Eighteen notations," Rin said. Not preamble. The way you report to someone who already knows the context. "Civic utilities documents across infrastructure, water, building maintenance records."

Mara waited.

"The pattern is different from yours." Rin was looking at the small trees in their October state. "Not individual people, mostly. The gaps are larger — what's missing isn't a person's record. It's a whole program. Civic infrastructure coordination networks from Year -3 to Year -1. Community resource programs that don't appear in any post-Transition record but are referenced, obliquely, in infrastructure reports from before. Entire systems quietly dissolved in Year 1, without administrative documentation, as though administration was not the point of dissolving them."

Mara considered this. An individual person erased from records left a specific absence — a name not in the index, a cross-reference pointing at nothing, a file that existed as a header with nothing inside. What Rin was describing was the scale above that. Not a person but a program. Not a record but a function — something that had existed to do something for people, that had done that thing, and that was now absent as though it had never done it. The evidence was not in the gap where a name should be. It was in the gap where a program's downstream effects should have appeared and didn't.

"That's a different silence," Mara said.

"Yes." Rin turned a page in the transit bag without looking at it, a gesture that wasn't reading. "The people who ran those programs — some of them may be in your records. But the programs themselves are structural erasures. The kind that read as standard administrative consolidation unless you're doing the cross-referencing."

They sat without speaking. The wind moved through the garden from the north, the amber trees shifting slightly.

"Davi is asking questions again," Rin said.

Mara did not respond immediately. She let the sentence settle.

"Not about the documentation method," Rin said. "Just asking. In transit coordination — he's been reviewing scheduling records from Year 2 and Year 3 against the current route maps. There are patterns that don't match. Certain routes eliminated in Year 2 served the outer zones, the less-registered areas. The official record gives no reason. The resource utilization logic doesn't explain it either."

"Has he found the cross-references?"

"He hasn't connected it yet. But he's asking why the routes were eliminated. He's asking colleagues, quietly." Rin paused. "He's the kind of person who doesn't let a thing go once it's bothered him."

Mara knew the kind. She was the kind. The question that lodged in the part of you that kept count — that waited there without resolving, that you went to work with every morning, that was still there when you came home. You either learned to carry it or you didn't. If you carried it long enough you stopped noticing the weight of it and just noticed what it had made you into, which was someone who asked carefully and continued anyway.

She did not say this. She sat with the trees in their October state and the transit lines overhead doing their regular work.

"He'll find his way to it," she said. "Or he won't."

"Yes," Rin said.

They did not stay long. The cold was real by mid-morning and neither of them was dressed for extended sitting. Rin stood first, adjusted the transit bag, looked north along the garden path. Mara stood. They made no arrangements for next time — the next time would arrive through the vendor, as this one had.

Rin went north. Mara went east.

The transit lines continued overhead, their intervals unchanged by any of this.

---

The card arrived at the vendor on a Thursday, and she knew before she read it that it was different from the slips she usually received. Not a slip — folded card stock, thicker, the fold made once rather than twice. The handwriting on the outside was not any handwriting she had memorized from the regular exchange, but she had seen it before, once, briefly: a note at the bottom of an early communication received through a route that no longer existed. She had kept the shape of it.

She bought the grain. She walked to the transit station. She waited until she was in her seat, the train moving, before she opened it.

The card said:

*I've been released. Standard exit processing. I'm not writing. I don't know yet what I am. But I wanted you to know: I watched the broadcast. I was there. It was real. I'm still asking. I don't know what that means, but I'm still asking.*

No name. No return address. The handwriting was identification enough.

Mara read it twice. The second reading was not faster than the first. She folded it back along the single crease and put it in her coat pocket — the outer pocket, not the inner one where the notebook lived.

She looked out the window. The city moving past in its October state: the trees in the transit corridors at their mid-turn, the civic buildings in the clear grey light of a day that had been trying to decide about rain and had settled for overcast. She thought: Nyah is out. Not free — in Eir's city *free* meant something specific that this was not. Out of monitored housing. Out of the compliance officer assignment that structured the reintegration period. The period completed, the paperwork filed, the status formally changed. Out.

*Not writing.* That was the loss in it, stated plainly. Writing had been the whole vocation — the precision Nyah brought to it, the structural quality, the way a sentence in her hands was built for load. She had been a writer who could not write in this city and who had written anyway in the only remaining registers, and who had paid for the writing in the ways you paid for things you were not permitted to do. And now she was out and not writing, which was not the same as the not-writing of before. Before there had been the watching, the witness, the ongoing careful attention. Now the sentence was *I don't know yet what I am.*

Some losses took time to know the shape of. You could not see the whole shape while you were still inside the first year of it.

*I'm still asking. I don't know what that means, but I'm still asking.*

She did not write this in the notebook. She carried the card home in the outer pocket. She set it on the kitchen table when she came in, ate dinner, washed the bowl and the cup, moved through the evening's routines — the small calibrations of a domestic life organized around predictability. Before bed she came back to the kitchen and looked at the card on the table, which was sitting exactly where she'd set it, in the lamp's modest light, holding the fact of itself.

She picked it up and put it in the inner pocket of her winter coat, which hung by the door. The coat she now wore — the weight of October — had a deep inner pocket she used for the notebook and for the things that traveled with her through days: the things she kept because they were specific and real and because carrying them was a form of acknowledgment that the specific and real required.

The card fit. She left it there.

---

The next slip from Jonah came through the vendor on a Friday morning — not a letter this time, not the folded pages she had received in December of the previous year, but a single receipt-sized slip, the handwriting she knew now: the firm, slightly left-leaning hand of someone who writes with deliberate pressure and economy of space, who has learned over fifteen years to say things briefly because brevity was the shape safety took.

She read it at lunch. Tomas was at the commissary.

*She was assigned pre-Transition civic planning records review. Her first evaluation cycle. She flagged three documents with notation queries — all standard, all filed correctly. She's learning the syntax. She doesn't know what she's learning.*

She put the slip down. She picked it up and read it again.

Three documents. Notation queries — standard archival procedure, the kind any careful archivist performed when a cross-index reference pointed at an absent record, when a cited source could not be verified, when a document's internal consistency required a flag. Three flags, all filed correctly, all within the parameters of ordinary professional practice. Unremarkable. Routine. The kind of queries that arrived in verification queues across the archive system on any given day, logged and assigned and addressed or deferred or held.

Except that they were not unremarkable if you knew what she had been doing when she filed them. Each one was a question, asked in the only formal register available to someone in that position, about a record that was absent or inconsistent or both. Three questions, correctly formed, correctly filed. She had found the form. She was using the form without knowing that the form was the beginning of something.

*Learning the syntax.* Mara had learned it too, before she understood what it was for — had learned to read the index structure, to trace the cross-references, to follow the footnote chains back to their source and note when the source was absent. Had learned to file the flag in the precise language that gave it legitimate existence in the record, that made it a documented fact of the archive rather than a private suspicion. The syntax was the house the questions lived in. You learned the house before you knew you were building it.

Jonah's daughter was twenty-six. She had filed three notation queries in her first evaluation cycle. She had asked, in the only language the record would hold, why something was missing.

Mara thought about this for a while. She did not try to hold all of it at once — Jonah, fifteen years of carrying an absence; his daughter in a planning office with a document in front of her, flagging a range; the flag sitting in a queue somewhere, routine, waiting to be addressed or archived.

She folded the slip and put it in the inner pocket of her winter coat, alongside the card.

---

On Wednesday evening the notebook was open on the kitchen table.

She had taken it from her bag at seven, set it beside the tea, sat down. The lamp at its evening angle. The window holding the city in the particular state of early November: the last of the corridor trees stripped clean, the outdoor temperature requiring the coat, the streets below carrying the quiet of a city that had been through its October adjustment and knew now what the winter would ask of it.

She looked at the names column.

Six entries, the column running down the back page in the small hand she used for names — slower than her verification hand, more deliberate. Elena Voss. Cael Marr. Arlan Sou. Cael Marr's mother, listed without a surname because the surname wasn't hers to assign, only the relationship and the year and the nature of the question she had been asking. And below those four: two more lines in the same hand, added in August's heat.

The fifth name. The person from the outer districts who had organized what the official record had no word for because the official record had dissolved the category. She knew from Sev's account that this person had been forty-three in Year 1, that they had known the sector with the particular intimacy of someone who had spent years learning its specific needs and building the specific structures that served them. She did not know what they had looked like or how they had talked. She knew three facts: an age, a vocation, a year. These were small facts. They were facts, and they were all she had, and the column held them now.

The sixth name. The person who had attended a gathering in Year -2 with forty-one others, whose name appeared on the list Ilsa had found in a fragile physical document, and who did not appear in any other record she or Ilsa had been able to locate. Forty-one names had continuity. This one did not. A gap of one in a list of forty-two, in a document that had survived because it was physical, because it had not been cleaned from a collection no one had recently prioritized, because Ilsa had been cataloguing that collection in the August heat with the steady patience she brought to everything. She knew one thing about this person: they had been present at something in Year -2. And she knew they had a name, which was on the list, which was now in the column.

She thought about what it meant to be in a column like this. Not testimony — she was not qualified to give that. Not memorial in the formal sense. Something smaller: acknowledgment. A record that said *this person existed and this is what I know.* She had said it six times now. The column held the count of it.

She closed the notebook.

The tea had gone lukewarm. She drank it anyway. She sat in her mother's sweater with the cup in her hands and the city outside the window in its managed evening — the transit pods on their reduced frequency, the civic buildings in their standard illumination, the ventilation sound and the occasional passing vehicle and below all of it the ambient background hum of a city governed to function at specific efficiency levels, a city she had been inside for seven years and which she could now read like weather.

She thought: the count would continue. The names column might grow again, if the right evidence arrived. Rin was at eighteen notations and had found a whole layer of structural erasure that intersected with Mara's individual records without being the same kind of thing — two kinds of silence in the same archive, legible if you knew to look. Jonah's daughter had filed three notation queries in her first evaluation cycle. Nyah was out and still asking, without knowing yet what asking meant for the life she was trying to reassemble. And the eighty-four people asking, twelve of whom had arrived by routes she hadn't opened and couldn't trace — she had not re-counted them and did not know whether the number had grown since June, and she did not need to know. The number was not the point. What was distributed could not be undone by counting it from one position.

She thought about the full shape of what she was in — not the shape she had imagined from inside Year -1, which had been no shape at all, only the first question she couldn't release and the compliance center after it and the fourteen months of reconstruction. The shape now was more visible from where she stood. Its pace was clear: the pace of documents, of working days, of people who could not release an inconsistency once they had found one. Its likely horizon was not her horizon but something longer, built to outlast the person building it. Its dimensions were wider than she had planned and were wider still because they extended into places she could not see — into Rin's notation work, into Ilsa's shelves, into a planning office where a careful twenty-six-year-old had filed three questions in their first evaluation cycle without knowing what she was filing them into.

She could see this. She could see it clearly, and see that it would continue without her being able to verify that it was continuing, and see that this was not a failure of architecture but a feature of it. And she could, with that full view, go to work in the morning and open the queue and do the next document. And the one after that.

Which was all you could do.

Which was — sitting here in her mother's sweater with the tea gone cold and the city outside in its ordered November evening — enough. Not in the defiant sense, not in the sense of making a virtue of limitation. In the other sense: the sense in which something is sufficient to its purpose, which was a different kind of enough and the only kind she trusted.

She got up. She rinsed the cup. She put the notebook in her bag, where it would be in the morning. She turned off the lamp.

The window held the city in the dark for a moment — the transit lines, the civic illumination, the small moving lights of the pods — and then she moved away from it and went to bed.

She slept without difficulty, which was not something she had always been able to do and which she noted, as she did most nights, with something close to gratitude.
